Summary

A 6-month turn-based tactics game built in Unreal Engine 5 at BUas. As lead producer of a 28-person multidisciplinary team, I owned the full production lifecycle, from concepting and pre-production planning through to external outsourcing and full production.

  • Team leadership: led a 28-person team across design, art and programming. Defined decision authority through a RACI matrix to enable leads to lead and teams to execute.
  • Scrum production: managed the product backlog in Jira; ran 2–3 week sprints with daily stand-ups, build reviews and retrospectives.
  • Resource planning: built a comprehensive production plan mapping the full production phase, scope, timeline and resource allocation.
  • Stakeholder communication: navigated a design pivot from time travel to dimensional travel, communicating the change clearly across team and stakeholders.
  • Conflict mediation: resolved scope and creative disputes between designers and programmers, maintaining team alignment and motivation.
  • Outsourcing: secured a live big-band recording through a multi-party partnership with the Breda Big Band, an external composer and a professional studio.
Producer work

On Project Heist

01Clear ownership

From the start, I knew 28 people needed clear ownership at every level. I built a RACI matrix around a simple principle: I enable leads to lead, and teams to execute. I held accountability for project health, schedule, and scope; discipline leads owned quality and creative direction; team members had autonomy over execution decisions.

To keep everyone aligned, I ran 2–3 week sprints around the academic calendar, managed the backlog in Jira, and maintained living documentation in Confluence. Daily stand-ups, build reviews, retrospectives, and cross-discipline meetings kept the rhythm steady and dependencies from becoming bottlenecks.

02Producing the concepting phase

The challenge: turn a team of up to 28 people, many with no concepting or cross-discipline experience, into an aligned game concept with a playable proof of fun, in three weeks.

Large teams spiral during open-ended brainstorming, so I designed a structured three-week concepting framework using game jams as a concepting tool. Rather than debating ideas in the abstract, teams built micro-prototypes to discover what works through doing.

Week 1 focused on exploration, reference analysis and short jams producing multiple micro-prototypes. Week 2 converged on a final direction, producing a full concept document with pillars, core loop, and proof of fun scope. Week 3 was a focused production sprint with daily stand-ups, a midweek playtest, and a hard deadline, scope protected with a built-in 20–30% buffer.

The framework also included structured decision-making guidelines, when to use small groups vs. full-team discussions, and explicit communication norms for feedback. I shared the concepting timeline with other producers leading parallel teams, making it a reusable framework beyond our project.

03Navigating a design pivot

Midway through pre-production, our core time-travel mechanic wasn't working. I identified the risk early, facilitated design discussions, and drove the team toward a decision between two directions: rework it into localised time travel, or pivot to dimensional travel while keeping our existing architecture intact.

I made the call to go with dimensional travel, the pragmatic choice that avoided a costly rework while opening new creative space for narrative and world-building. I then led the communication of the pivot to stakeholders and the full team, ensuring everyone understood the reasoning and what it meant for their work going forward.

04Scope & collaboration

Early in the project, designers wanted to keep scope small for quality, leaving programmers underutilised and the two disciplines struggling to collaborate. I intervened early, bringing both sides together to map out the actual workload and estimate effort together.

Through structured mediation, I helped programmers identify meaningful contributions — level editors, QA automation, and iteration tools — while giving designers clarity on what programmer support could unlock. It took ongoing facilitation and lead check-ins to stick, but the result was a team where both disciplines felt ownership and purpose.

05Securing a live big-band recording

I set out to secure live big-band jazz for the soundtrack, the perfect fit for a heist game. What I scoped as 8 hours turned into 24+ across multiple weeks, as what seemed like "send some emails, get a yes" revealed itself to be a full multi-party outsourcing operation.

I was managing communication across four external parties simultaneously: the Breda Big Band and their conductor, composer Samuel Wright from The International School of The Hague, a professional recording studio, and academic stakeholders for funding approval. Each had their own timelines, availability, and decision-making processes, requiring me to track dependencies, chase responses, compile pricing options, explore alternative funding routes, and keep momentum alive across all fronts at once.

The result: first samples delivered, recording studio booked, and Project Heist will ship with live-recorded big-band music.

Lesson learned

“External dependencies don't add overhead, they multiply it. Every additional party adds more waiting, more coordination, and a greater risk of the whole chain stalling. I now scope outsourcing based on the number of parties involved, rather than just the task's apparent complexity.”

▼

06Production planning

One of my key contributions was building a comprehensive production plan and resource allocation for the entire production phase. It mapped out what needed to happen, when, and with which people, across all disciplines.

Having this plan gives the team clarity and enables me to anticipate bottlenecks before they occur. It has become the backbone of our sprint planning and helped discipline leads understand not only their own roadmap but also how their work connects to the rest of the project.

Personal growth

Project Heist is pushing me further than any project before, not just in scale, but in the complexity of what production leadership actually demands.

  • I learned that outsourcing is its own discipline, requiring patience, relationship management, and a fundamentally different approach to scoping than internal work.
  • I strengthened my ability to facilitate difficult conversations between disciplines with competing needs, finding solutions that respect both sides.
  • I gained experience in production planning at scale, mapping resources, dependencies, and timelines for a 28-person team across a 6-month project.
  • I deepened my conviction that clear structure enables creative freedom. When people know who decides what, they spend less energy on politics and more on their work.
