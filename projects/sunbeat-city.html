On Sunbeat City

01From six people to twenty-one

In early 2025, I stepped into my biggest challenge yet: producing Sunbeat City, a 16-week game project that started with just six people — five designers and me, working as both designer and producer.

The goal for the first eight weeks was simple in theory, brutal in practice: create a playable prototype good enough to pitch to the lecturing staff. Only the most promising projects would make it through to full production.

We made it. And in the process, my role transformed completely.

02Scaling up

After the pitch, our team exploded in size: twenty-one designers, artists, and programmers, plus me as the sole producer. My job shifted from balancing two roles to managing both the product and the people.

We had eight weeks to deliver a polished vertical slice for industry review. That meant building a production pipeline that could keep a large, multi-disciplinary team focused and aligned. For this goal I had to prepare a lot of documentation so our team could work as smoothly as possible, including a scope breakdown, dependency mappings, and other production documentation.

03A process that empowered the team

I created a planning approach I called Autonomous Agile, based on Scrum, but with more room for personal ownership.

Instead of assigning rigid sprint tasks, each developer took full responsibility for their own user stories, planning their own timelines while working toward shared deadlines. To keep things organised, I used Codecks to track tasks, and ran:

  • Daily stand-ups for quick syncs.
  • Build reviews to assess quality.
  • Retrospectives to improve process over time.

The result? The team stayed connected without feeling micromanaged.

04Collaboration across disciplines

With a team this large, collaboration had to be deliberate.

I set up cross-discipline meetings so leads and team members could surface blockers early. I also made time for one-on-one check-ins — especially important for those less comfortable speaking up in groups.

Over time, the team shifted from "school project" mode to feeling like a professional game team. People began giving and receiving feedback openly, regardless of discipline, and collaboration felt natural rather than forced.

05The hard stuff — conflict management

Not everything was smooth.

Midway through production, one teammate's behaviour was negatively affecting others. I initially tried to limit their impact by assigning them more isolated work, but the issues persisted. Multiple people shared that they felt undermined or pressured by this person's approach.

Looking back, I should have addressed it directly much sooner, setting clearer boundaries and expectations early on.

Lesson learned

“Producing isn't just about planning; it's about protecting the team's focus and well-being.”

▼

06Quality assurance

Our QA process, built on smoke testing and a clear agreement, kept the game stable. But as the project evolved, people started testing differently than documented, and I didn't update the official process to match.

While this didn't cause major problems, it was a reminder that process must reflect reality, and that consistency matters when quality is on the line.

07Publishing & community

One of the project's highlights was our publishing strategy.

From the start, we planned an early access release, using TikTok, Instagram, Reddit, and Bluesky to reach players. We were transparent that this was a public playtest, and that honesty encouraged valuable, actionable feedback — which I was in charge of gathering and communicating back to the team.

By the time we officially launched, we'd already addressed key issues, polished core features, and refined what players enjoyed most. The final release was stronger because of it.

Personal growth

Sunbeat City taught me that producing is about people first, process second — and that the two are inseparable.

I left with a sharper eye for production design, a stronger understanding of leadership, and a clear determination to keep growing. Whether leading a small creative team or managing a large multi-disciplinary project, my goal is the same: build a structure where people can do their best work — and feel proud of it.

This project pushed me to grow in ways I hadn't expected:

  • I learned to delegate more and avoid overextending myself.
  • I saw the value of clear structure and accountability, even when it risks short-term discomfort.
  • I developed a deeper appreciation for individual skills beyond job titles.
  • I identified gaps in my knowledge, especially in areas like level design and animation, that I want to close.

It also shifted my career aspirations. Through industry connections, I've set my sights on producing at Remedy Entertainment in Finland, and this project gave me the confidence to believe I can get there.
